The rain had been following Geralt for three days. It was not the violent kind that came with thunder and frightened horses, but a thin, cold, persistent rain that soaked slowly through leather, wool, patience, and eventually temper.
Geralt sat heavily in the saddle and pulled his cloak farther over one shoulder. Water had already found its way beneath the collar anyway. He glanced at Roach’s wet mane. “Don’t look at me like that. I didn’t summon it.” The mare flicked one ear backward without slowing. “You’re under a saddle blanket.” Roach snorted. Geralt sighed. “Fine. We’re both miserable.” The road south of the Pontar had become a long ribbon of mud. Geralt had intended to reach a larger town before nightfall, somewhere with a stable, a blacksmith, and preferably an innkeeper who understood that watered beer was still supposed to contain some beer. He had also intended to eat before sunset.
Neither plan survived the weather. The village appeared shortly before dusk, half-hidden behind dripping birch trees. A weathered board beside the road announced its name as Ravelin, though one letter had fallen away and another hung upside down. Geralt studied it for a moment. “Promising.” Perhaps thirty houses surrounded a muddy square.
Smoke rose reluctantly from stone chimneys, and a small shrine to Melitele stood beneath an old oak, its offerings protected from the rain by a crude wooden roof. Beside it was an inn whose sign depicted a red stag with an expression of astonishing intelligence. A farmer carrying a broken basket stopped when he noticed the swords on Geralt’s back. His face passed through recognition, suspicion, and finally the particular sort of hope people often felt embarrassed to show in front of a witcher. Geralt saw all three and looked toward the inn. He could already smell onions frying in fat. He was wet, hungry, and in no mood to bargain over drowners for the price of two chickens. The farmer followed him anyway. His boots made wet sucking sounds in the mud until Geralt finally turned in the saddle. “If you’re going to ask, ask.”
The man stopped. “You are one, then.” “Depends what you mean by one.” “A witcher.” Geralt looked at him for a moment. “Unfortunately.” The farmer blinked, uncertain whether that had been a joke. “We’ve got coin.” “Good for you.” “Something’s killing our dogs.” Geralt looked back at him. The smell of the inn suddenly mattered less. “People?” “Not yet.” Geralt disliked those two words together. “How many dogs?” “Six in two weeks. Maybe seven now. And Old Beren’s goat. Tore them open, hardly ate anything.”
“Wolves?” “Mayor says so.” “And you?” The farmer lowered his voice. “Wolves don’t leave dogs hanging from trees.” Geralt’s expression changed. “No. They don’t.” The farmer let out a breath. “Will you look?” “After I eat.” “Of course.” Geralt dismounted and handed him Roach’s reins. “She gets oats. Dry ones.” The farmer stared at the horse. “She?”
Geralt gave him a flat look. “Dry oats,” the man repeated quickly. Inside the inn, conversation weakened as soon as Geralt entered. He was accustomed to that particular silence. It rarely lasted long. People enjoyed pretending not to stare almost as much as they enjoyed staring. He removed his wet gloves, chose a table against the wall, and sat with a quiet grunt. His knees ached from riding, and an old scar beneath his left shoulder had begun complaining several hours earlier, as it always did in cold rain. A young serving woman approached. She could not have been more than seventeen, though she carried herself with the determined seriousness of someone who had been told repeatedly not to be afraid. Her eyes moved toward the silver sword. Geralt noticed and softened his voice. “Whatever’s hot.” “Stew.” “Good.” She waited. Geralt raised an eyebrow. “What kind?” He glanced toward the kitchen. “The hot kind.” For a moment she stared at him, then a laugh escaped before she could stop it.
Geralt lowered his eyes toward the table, hiding the faintest movement at the corner of his mouth. “Rabbit or cabbage?” she asked. “Does the rabbit contain rabbit?” “Mostly.” “Rabbit.” “Beer?”
Geralt looked through the window at the rain. “Two.” By the time the stew arrived, three men had found reasons to stand near his table. Geralt ignored them and ate. One finally cleared his throat. Geralt continued eating. The man did it again, louder. “If you keep doing that,” Geralt said without looking up, “you should see a healer.” A few people nearby laughed.
The man’s face darkened. He was broad, thick-necked, and wore a chain bearing the village seal. “I’m Mayor Holt.” Geralt looked up. “Congratulations.” The mayor pulled out the opposite chair and sat. “Marek says you’re interested in our problem.” “Marek said something is killing animals. I said wolves don’t hang dogs in trees. Those are different statements.”
“So you’ll deal with it.” “I haven’t seen anything yet.” “That’s what witchers do, isn’t it?” Geralt put down his spoon. He had heard variations of that sentence in more towns than he cared to remember. People always seemed remarkably certain about what witchers did, right up until they were asked to pay for it. “Witchers identify monsters. Then, if there is a monster, we negotiate a price. Then I decide whether killing it is possible. Preferably in that order.” “There’s always a price with your kind.” “Blacksmiths charge for horseshoes.” “That’s different.”
“Of course.” The serving girl placed Geralt’s second mug on the table with slightly more force than necessary. “Let the man eat, Uncle.” The mayor frowned. “Lena.” “You complained for ten days that no one would come. Now someone came.” “Back to the kitchen.”
Lena’s jaw tightened, but she left. Geralt watched her go. “Her dog?” Holt looked at him. “What?” “One of the dead ones.” The mayor’s expression changed just enough. “Bran. Black shepherd. She raised him from a pup.” Geralt drank from his mug. He knew what people sounded like when they talked about an animal they had lost while trying to convince themselves that grieving for it was foolish. “Where did you find him?” “North of the village.
Alder grove.” “And the others?” “Same area.” “Anyone hear anything?” “A scream sometimes.” “Human?” Holt hesitated. “Sounds like a woman.” Geralt leaned back slightly. “Anything else?” “No.” “You’re sure?”
“Yes.” Geralt watched him. Holt shifted in the chair. “What?” “You hesitated.” “I was thinking.” “You were deciding whether to lie.” The mayor stared at him, then looked toward the kitchen. “Bran’s heart was gone.” Geralt’s face became still. “And you didn’t think to mention that?” “I didn’t know it mattered.” “A dog was hanging from a tree with its chest opened and its heart missing.” “I’m not a witcher.”
“No,” Geralt said. “You’re definitely not.” A short while later, Lena was waiting beside the stable with a lantern. “I’m coming.” Holt stopped dead behind Geralt. “No.” “Bran was mine.” “And that’s exactly why you’re staying here.” “You said wolves for ten days. You kept saying wolves while you burned the bodies. If you’d listened earlier, maybe Bran would still be alive.” The mayor flinched. Geralt lowered his gaze. He had learned, badly and much later than he liked to admit, that there were arguments where silence was wisdom and others where it was simply cowardice. Lena looked at him. “You believe me, don’t you?”
“I believe wolves don’t perform surgery.” Her face tightened. “What took his heart?” “Too many things.” “Tell me one.” “No.” “Why?” “Because then you’ll choose the one that scares you most and convince yourself it’s outside every window.”
Lena looked irritated. “That sounds like something you’ve seen people do before.” Geralt took the lantern from her hand. “Frequently.” “I’m still coming.” “No.” “You don’t decide that.”
“True. Whatever killed Bran decides it the moment you enter the grove.” Her anger faltered. Geralt handed the lantern back to her. “Tell me exactly how you found him.” Lena swallowed. “His chest was open. There wasn’t much blood. His paws were muddy, but the ground under the tree wasn’t. And…” She hesitated. “And?”
“He smelled strange.” “How?” “Sweet. Like flowers. But rotten too.” Geralt’s eyes narrowed. “What kind of flowers?” “I don’t know.” “Lilac?” Lena thought for a moment. “No. Something heavier.” Geralt looked away. For an instant, memory supplied another scent without invitation: lilac and gooseberries, tangled with black curls, impatience, arguments, warmth, and several mistakes he had at different times sworn never to repeat. He pushed the thought aside before it could settle. Holt noticed the pause. “Does that mean something?”
“Everything means something. Usually not what people hope.” Lena hugged the lantern closer. “Will you kill it?” Geralt looked at her. He hated that question, mostly because people asked it as if the world reliably divided itself into things that deserved killing and things that did not. He had spent much of his life being paid by people who believed the distinction obvious. It almost never was. “First I’ll find out what it is.”
“And then?” “Then I’ll decide.” The alder grove stood less than half a mile from Ravelin. The rain had stopped, but droplets continued to fall from the branches. Wet leaves, mushrooms, rotting wood, and something faintly metallic scented the air.
Geralt stopped just inside the trees and raised one hand. Holt nearly walked into him. “What?” Geralt crouched beside a patch of disturbed mud. “Tracks.” “Wolf?” “No.” “What, then?”
Geralt pointed. Bare human footprints crossed the mud. Small ones. “A child?” Holt whispered. Geralt did not answer. He followed the tracks deeper into the grove, twenty paces, thirty, then stopped beneath an alder tree. Another dog hung from a branch. Holt made a choking sound. “Gods.” The animal had been killed recently. Its chest was open, but the ribs had not been torn apart. They had been cut. Geralt examined the wound, then the ground beneath it. Almost no blood.
“No claws,” he said. Holt stood several steps behind him. “What does that mean?” “No teeth either.” “You said a monster was doing this.” “I said wolves weren’t.”
The mayor swallowed. Geralt moved around the tree and found a thin red circle around the trunk. He touched it, smelled his fingers, and frowned. Blood.
Above it, another mark had been carved into the bark: two intersecting curves surrounding a short vertical line. Holt came closer. “You know that symbol?” “Part of it.” “Part?” Geralt looked at the carving. “Which is more than I’d like.” He reached beneath his shirt and drew out the wolf medallion resting against his chest. It trembled. Only slightly, but unmistakably. Holt took a step back. “Magic.” “Probably.”
“Probably?” “Certainty is expensive.” “I thought witchers knew these things.” Geralt glanced at him. “I thought mayors knew where their dogs’ hearts went.” Holt wisely said nothing. Then a child began crying somewhere deeper in the grove. Both men turned. A little girl’s voice drifted through the trees, perhaps six or seven years old, calling for her mother. Holt whispered, “There are no houses this way.” “I know.” “We should help her.” Geralt caught the mayor’s arm before he could move. “No.” “But—”
“Listen.” The crying stopped. Silence filled the grove. Then the same voice returned from somewhere much closer. “Please.” Holt went pale. Geralt’s hand moved toward his sword. “Please,” the child repeated. “It’s dark.” Holt whispered, “What is that?” Geralt drew steel. “Stay behind me.” The voice changed. Not slowly. Not naturally. One moment it belonged to a frightened child. The next it was a woman’s voice, low and calm. “Geralt.” He stopped breathing for half a second. Holt stared at him. “It knows you.”
Geralt said nothing. “Geralt of Rivia.” The trees were still. No wind. No footsteps. Holt backed away. “How does it know your name?” Geralt drew the silver sword with his other hand. For several seconds he stood beneath the dripping branches with steel in one hand and silver in the other. He disliked fighting with both. It meant he had insufficient information, and insufficient information was generally what got witchers killed. The woman’s voice laughed softly from somewhere ahead.
Geralt felt an old, irrational wish that another familiar woman were standing nearby to tell him, with brutal precision, exactly how stupid his present situation was. Yennefer would have managed it in fewer than ten words. Possibly five. The thought irritated him. The fact that it comforted him irritated him more. The child began crying again. Geralt’s mouth hardened. “Go back to Ravelin.” Holt looked at him. “What?” “Tell everyone to stay indoors. Shut the shutters. Lock the doors.
If they hear someone calling from outside, they don’t answer.” “And you?” Geralt rolled one aching shoulder. He was wet, tired, hungry again, and increasingly certain that his original plan of drinking mediocre beer beside a warm fire had been the best idea he had possessed all week. He looked into the darkness between the trees.
“I’m going for a walk.” Something moved ahead. The wolf medallion began to shake.
